## A level belongs to a technique

It is tempting to call someone simply a beginner, intermediate, or advanced player. That is useful shorthand, but it hides the interesting part. Table tennis players do not develop every skill at the same rate.

This training system assigns levels **by technique**. Someone might be advanced at backhand blocking, intermediate at serving, and a beginner at forehand looping against backspin. That is not a contradiction. It is a much more useful diagnosis than one global label, because it tells us what to practice next.

The three levels below describe a progression for a particular technique. A player can occupy different levels in different parts of their game.

## Before the levels: the true beginner foundation

Before it makes sense to grade a particular stroke, a new player needs a small foundation: a workable grip, an athletic stance, the four basic strokes, and a simple legal serve. The goal here is not to collect variations or make the serve deceptive. The goal is to make the fundamentals visible and repeatable.

Practice with a true beginner should make those fundamentals easier to see. Simple topspin and backspin are useful because the player can identify what happened. Complicated serves and heavy, disguised spin can wait. A rally that reveals a stable stance, a clear contact, or a basic stroke teaches more than a point won by confusion.

## Beginner: learn to make and read the ball

At the beginner level, the player starts to understand that the racket does more than send the ball forward. Brushing contact creates spin. The opponent's spin changes what the ball does after contact. These are separate ideas, and both need time.

For a technique at this level, useful goals include:

- learning how brushing changes the ball
- recognizing and defending against heavier spin
- judging whether an incoming ball carries more or less spin
- developing one dependable serve variation

The practice itself should stay simple. Forehand to forehand and backhand to backhand drills make it easier to notice the movement. More elaborate drills earn their place when they isolate a real game problem, such as opening after a short backspin ball.

## Intermediate: use the technique with a purpose

An intermediate player has a functional version of the basic strokes and a dependable serve. The next step asks a harder question: can the player choose and use the technique in a realistic point?

That often means learning more demanding receives, opening against backspin, and deciding when forehand or backhand is the better reply. Forehand opening against backspin is a sensible first example because it makes the relationship between spin, footwork, and racket path very clear.

Strategy also becomes concrete here. A player can ask which serve produces the return they want, or which receive moves the rally toward a stronger wing. Matches become useful for finding these patterns. They reveal which of the four basic strokes holds up under pressure and which one gives away points.

## Advanced: improve quality and fit

At the advanced level, players can use higher-level versions of familiar skills: opening against backspin, countering topspin, or blocking different spins with control. The work now depends more strongly on the game the player wants to play.

There are several worthwhile directions:

- make the ball better: more reliable placement, spin, speed, depth, or height control
- sharpen tactics: choose patterns that bring the rally toward preferred positions
- change the toolkit: add, refine, or occasionally remove a technique so the game fits the player's style

The important point is that advanced work does not merely mean making every stroke more complicated. It means improving the strokes and decisions that matter for the player's actual game.

## A few rules for every level

Consistency remains the standard. A beautiful shot that appears once is a clue; a shot that appears on demand is a tool.

Training should also mirror a plausible game state. If a drill asks a player to block a ball from a position that would not occur in a point, it may teach an answer to the wrong question. Beginning drills from a serve helps keep the first ball, the expected return, and the next decision connected.

Finally, when a technique feels unusually hard or requires a great deal of force, check the feet and stance first. Poor footwork often makes a fundamentally sound racket motion feel impossible. A quieter, more balanced setup usually gives the stroke room to work.

## Using the system

Choose one technique, place it honestly, and define the next small capability. For example: “my backhand push is intermediate because I can control ordinary backspin, but I need to read variation better.” That gives practice a target without pretending that the rest of the game has the same level.

The system is meant to guide practice, not to rank people. A player can be learning one technique while relying on another. That unevenness is normal, and it gives training its direction.
